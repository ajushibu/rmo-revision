    Why this check exists

    A bicarbonate of 24 looks perfectly normal. But in a patient with a high anion gap it can hide two problems at once. The trick is to predict where the bicarbonate should be, then compare it with the real value.

    1. An acid is two parts: a hydrogen ion (H+) and an anion that came with it, for example lactate or a ketone.
    2. The hydrogen ion eats one bicarbonate. It turns it into CO2 and water. So every bit of acid that arrives lowers the bicarbonate by about one.
    3. The anion is left behind. No lab test counts it, so it shows up as the anion gap. The gap is normally about 12, so the extra gap (gap minus 12) is how much acid arrived.
    4. So predict: a normal bicarbonate of 24, minus the extra gap, is where it should be if acid is the only problem. Then compare with the real value.

    Two things have to happen

    A healthy kidney dumps spare bicarbonate easily. So a metabolic alkalosis that lasts needs two things: something that starts it (bicarbonate goes up), and something that stops the kidney dumping it (so it stays). Think of the blood bicarbonate as water in a tank, and the kidney as the drain.

    Alkalosis tab. The reasons given (acid left behind, sodium kept with bicarbonate when chloride is short, potassium and aldosterone) are standard physiology teaching. I did not check them against an Australian source here, and the picture shows direction only, not measured values.

    How far to trust this. The pH values are calculated from hydrogen ion (nmol/L) = 24 x pCO2 / bicarbonate. The bicarbonate change per 10 mmHg of CO2 uses mid-range values: acidosis 1 acute and 3.5 chronic, alkalosis 2 acute and 4.5 chronic (Merck Manual ranges are 1 to 2 and 3 to 4 for acidosis, 1 to 2 and 4 to 5 for alkalosis). Real patients vary and the time course is a smooth teaching curve, not measured data.

    The anion gap here has a normal of 12 at an albumin of 40 g/L and falls 0.25 for every 1 g/L of albumin lost (2.5 per g/dL). I could not check these against an Australian laboratory reference; some labs include potassium and quote a different range.
