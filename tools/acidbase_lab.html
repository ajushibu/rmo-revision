    Where should the bicarbonate be, and where does it come out?

    Two things have to happen

    A healthy kidney dumps spare bicarbonate easily. So a metabolic alkalosis that lasts needs two things: something that starts it (bicarbonate goes up), and something that stops the kidney dumping it (so it stays). Think of the blood bicarbonate as water in a tank, and the kidney as the drain.

    Why? (what you picked)

    One question: is the kidney short of chloride?

    The kidney's first job is to protect the body's volume. It does that by keeping sodium, and sodium cannot be kept alone: it needs a negative partner, and the main one is chloride. So how much chloride the urine lets go tells you whether the body is short of it.

    Pick a patient

    Why is the chloride low in vomiting?

    1. Stomach acid is hydrochloric acid (HCl): hydrogen ions and chloride leave the blood together to make it.
    2. When the stomach contents are vomited or sucked out, the chloride goes with them. The blood is left short of chloride.
    3. The blood must stay balanced (sodium on one side, chloride plus bicarbonate plus the rest on the other). With less chloride, bicarbonate takes its place, so it rises.
    4. Water and sodium are lost too, so the volume falls. Now the kidney is short of both, and clings to everything it can.

    What the names mean

    Chloride-responsive: the alkalosis goes away when you give chloride (as saline). The body was short of chloride, so it responds to getting it. Urine chloride is low.

    Chloride-resistant: giving chloride does nothing, because chloride was never the problem. Something else is driving it (aldosterone, severe potassium loss). Urine chloride is high.

    Alkalosis and chloride tabs. The reasons given (acid left behind, sodium kept with bicarbonate when chloride is short, potassium and aldosterone) are standard physiology teaching. I did not check them against an Australian source here, and the picture shows direction only, not measured values.

    How far to trust this. The pH values are calculated from hydrogen ion (nmol/L) = 24 x pCO2 / bicarbonate. The bicarbonate change per 10 mmHg of CO2 uses mid-range values: acidosis 1 acute and 3.5 chronic, alkalosis 2 acute and 4.5 chronic (Merck Manual ranges are 1 to 2 and 3 to 4 for acidosis, 1 to 2 and 4 to 5 for alkalosis). Real patients vary and the time course is a smooth teaching curve, not measured data.

    The anion gap here has a normal of 12 at an albumin of 40 g/L and falls 0.25 for every 1 g/L of albumin lost (2.5 per g/dL). I could not check these against an Australian laboratory reference; some labs include potassium and quote a different range.
