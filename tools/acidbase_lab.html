Three small pictures for the three things that were hard to see: where the anion gap comes from, how to find a hidden alkalosis, and why chronic CO2 retention looks milder than acute.

The two sides must balance

The positive side (sodium) always equals the negative side. Chloride and bicarbonate are measured. Whatever is left over is the unmeasured anions: the anion gap. Pick what happens, then move the slider.

Where did the bicarbonate start?

In a high-gap acidosis each unit of extra gap used up about one unit of bicarbonate. Add the extra gap back to the measured bicarbonate to see where it started. Above normal means a metabolic alkalosis was there first.

The kidney needs days

The same CO2 gives a very different pH depending on how long the kidney has had to hold on to (or dump) bicarbonate. Slide the days from 0 (acute) to 5 (chronic).

How far to trust this. The pH values are calculated from hydrogen ion (nmol/L) = 24 x pCO2 / bicarbonate. The bicarbonate change per 10 mmHg of CO2 uses mid-range values: acidosis 1 acute and 3.5 chronic, alkalosis 2 acute and 4.5 chronic (Merck Manual ranges are 1 to 2 and 3 to 4 for acidosis, 1 to 2 and 4 to 5 for alkalosis). Real patients vary and the time course is a smooth teaching curve, not measured data.

The anion gap here has a normal of 12 at an albumin of 40 g/L and falls 0.25 for every 1 g/L of albumin lost (2.5 per g/dL). I could not check these against an Australian laboratory reference; some labs include potassium and quote a different range.
