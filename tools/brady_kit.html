How far to trust this tab. The atropine dose, adverse-sign list, adrenaline and pacing steps come from ANZCOR Guideline 11.9, whose page carries no revision date (the Australian Resuscitation Council version I found is dated November 2009). The glucagon dose and the sedation drug for pacing were not in the Australian sources, so I have left them out: take them from eTG or your hospital protocol. The atropine and MI caution comes from the US template and teaching, not from ANZCOR. Check the current ANZCOR page and your hospital's guideline before you act.

    What it means

    Causes to look for

    Drugs (beta-blockers, calcium-channel blockers, digoxin, amiodarone, opioids), hyperkalaemia, inferior MI, hypothyroidism, hypothermia, raised intracranial pressure, infection, high vagal tone. Check the drug chart, the potassium and the ECG first.

    Read the four heart blocks

    Blue marks are P waves (atria), dark spikes are QRS complexes (ventricles). Red dashes are dropped beats. Pick a block.

    Why atropine is a gamble in the dangerous ones

    Atropine speeds up the sinus node and the AV node. A block in the AV node (first degree, Mobitz I, many cases of inferior MI) still has a node to speed up, so it often works. A block below the node (Mobitz II, complete block with a broad QRS) is a wiring fault that atropine cannot reach, so go to pacing without waiting. A transplanted heart has no vagal supply, so atropine has nothing to remove.
