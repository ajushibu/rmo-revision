Low sodium: how the water goes wrong

Four small labs. Start with the first: it explains most of the rest.

How much water can the kidney get rid of?

The kidney has to pass the day's solute (salt, urea, protein breakdown) in some water. The most dilute urine it can make sets how much water it can lose. Drink more than that and the sodium falls.

A normal diet is about 700 mOsm. Tea and toast is very low.

50 = ADH off, 1200 = ADH on at maximum (stuck on in SIADH).

How fast may the sodium come up?

Brain cells adapt to a low sodium by pushing out their own solutes. That keeps them from swelling, but it means a fast rise can then shrink them.

A: the sodium is normal, the machine is wrong

Plasma is mostly water, with a little fat and protein. Most lab analysers measure sodium per volume of the whole sample, then assume the water share is normal (about 93%). Add a lot of fat or protein and there is less water in the sample, so the machine reads low while the sodium in the water is fine.

Normal is about 7%.

B: a lot of sugar drags water out of the cells

Glucose (or mannitol, or glycine) cannot get into cells. It holds water in the blood, which dilutes the sodium. The body's sodium has not changed. Here the serum osmolality is high, not low.

Corrected sodium (mmol/L)

The rule that sorts all of this

Send a serum osmolality. Low means the low sodium is real. Normal means pseudohyponatraemia (the machine). High means something is pulling water out of the cells (usually glucose).
