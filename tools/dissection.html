Aortic Dissection Classification

Stanford and DeBakey describe the same thing: how far the tear reaches along the thoracic aorta. The one question that sets treatment is whether the ascending aorta is involved.

Type A: ascending aorta involved

Stanford A = DeBakey I + II

  • Emergency cardiothoracic surgery is the treatment of choice.
  • Can spread to the coronary ostia (MI, inferior more often than left-sided), the aortic valve (regurgitation), the pericardium (tamponade) and the carotids (stroke).
  • While waiting: ICU, IV beta-blocker (esmolol or labetalol) first, then nitroprusside if needed. No anticoagulants or thrombolytics.
  • Untreated, nearly 75% die within 2 weeks. Peak age 50-55.

Type B: ascending aorta not involved

Stanford B = DeBakey III

  • Stable type B is treated medically: blood pressure and heart rate control, analgesia, ICU admission.
  • Surgery or intervention is for patients who fail medical therapy. The website's template adds endovascular repair (TEVAR) in selected complicated cases.
  • Complications to watch for: branch-vessel compromise (renal, mesenteric, spinal), expansion and rupture.
  • Peak age 60-70.

Source: Rosen and Barkin's 5-Minute Emergency Medicine Consult (5th ed., 2015), Aortic Dissection, Thoracic, PDF pages 413-418. Complication list for type B from the Point of Care Medicine aortic dissection template.

The book defines DeBakey I by where the tear starts (arch or root). Most references describe it by how far it spreads, which is how this figure draws it. If a question asks for the book's wording, use the book's. Confirm treatment thresholds against your hospital guideline.
